The challenge

The challenge

Just Eat faced a critical retention problem: users engaged sporadically, viewing the platform as a transactional utility rather than a valued Membership.

Order frequency was inconsistent, and the cost of re-acquiring lapsed users was rising. The business needed a structural intervention, not just a marketing campaign, to shift user mindset and build sustainable revenue through subscriptions.

Engagement

Paid-members with our competitors are spending 2–4× more and ordering 10–30% more than non-paid members — whilst exhibiting highly valuable behaviours (e.g. Grocery & Retail penetration).

Retention

13% of our lapsed customers have left due to joining a competitor's paid program (+4% year on year).

"Consumers primarily switched to competitor products because they offered more compelling deals and superior reward programs"

The goal

Shift users from "transactional" to "Membership-based" thinking.

The strategic goal was twofold: retain existing customers by giving them a tangible reason to stay, and acquire new high-value customers through a compelling, low-friction entry point. The design challenge was to make the value of membership feel immediate — not deferred — so users experienced the benefit before they had time to second-guess the decision.

The solution

Unlimited Free delivery partnership with Amazon Prime

We worked with commercial and marketing teams to design a partnership integration which would allow Amazon Prime members access to Unlimited Free Delivery via the Just Eat platform.

The trade-off

Amazon's login met Just Eat's markets. Something had to give.

Amazon authenticates through one global URL. Just Eat authenticates per market. There was no version of this flow where both architectures won — so the job was choosing which one bent, on purpose, and being able to defend it in a room with both companies in it.

Problem
A Prime member's single global session doesn't know which Just Eat market they belong to — Germany and Austria each run on separate login stacks.
Options weighed
Force a single global session, accept the security compromise Silently auto‑detect market from IP/locale, risk wrong‑market activation Add one explicit country‑selection step
Decision
I chose the extra step over the shortcut. Silent detection would occasionally activate the wrong market's benefit with no way for the user to notice until checkout — a support‑ticket generator dressed up as convenience.
Trade-off accepted
One additional screen in the happy path, in exchange for a login flow that never guesses on the user's behalf. I communicated that cost explicitly to both Amazon's and Just Eat's stakeholders before it shipped — not after someone noticed the extra tap in a usability read‑out.

Solving critical UX risks under a compressed timeline

A one‑line user story with a business model behind it

The trigger wasn't a design brief. It was a support ticket pattern: Just Eat+ members who were also Amazon Prime members, paying twice for the same delivery benefit without realising it.

"As a Just Eat+ member and an Amazon Prime member, I want to switch to my free Prime delivery benefit — and I want to be clear on the refund policy so I can make an informed decision."

— field note that started the account‑linking initiative

That one line implied the whole brief: two authentication systems, two loyalty ledgers, and a refund policy question that had to be answered before a single screen got designed. Underneath it sat the real shape of the project — two global brands, two markets, and one activation moment that had to work identically whether someone arrived from Amazon's app, Amazon's website, or a Just Eat account they'd had for six years.

Tension 01

Two identity systems, one moment

Amazon's single‑URL global login met Just Eat's per‑market URL structure — built for a different era of the product, never designed to hand off mid‑session to a partner.

Tension 02

Two markets, one flow

Germany and Austria each had their own login quirks and legacy screens. One activation flow had to absorb that inconsistency without the user ever seeing the seams.

Tension 03

Generosity vs. exploitation

The benefit had to be trivially easy to claim once — and impossible to claim twice, across guest sessions, duplicate accounts and cross‑app identities.

Switching to Prime

Switching to Prime

A segment of users had already paid for Just Eat+ on a one-time basis — and then discovered they were also eligible for free delivery through Amazon Prime. They were paying twice for the same benefit. The problem wasn't just giving them a way to switch; it was making the cancellation and refund policy clear enough that they could make a confident, informed decision before committing.

What I'd change next

Shipping it taught me where the seams still show

A few of these were conscious phase‑1 trade‑offs at the time. With activation volume behind us now, they read differently — as the next brief, not as mistakes.

01

Replace the generic error state with account‑specific diagnostics

"Something went wrong, it's not you, it's us" was a deliberate phase‑1 simplification, flagged as such on the original board. With real activation and failure data now available, it's worth the investment to tell a user which of the ten branches they actually hit.

02

Unify Just Eat's per‑market login screens

This flow was built to absorb that fragmentation rather than fix it — the right call under a three‑month deadline, but it means every future partner integration inherits the same legacy debt until One App/Web lands.

03

Soften the guest‑account warning into a merge, not a hard stop

The current pattern asks a guest user to accept data loss before continuing. A lighter merge flow — keep the guest basket, add the real account — would likely convert more of the DE/AT guest population, who hit this branch often.

04

Track every branch, not just the main flow

We set up analytics on the main activation path but didn't measure the edge cases with the same rigour. I know exactly what each error state and fallback branch does — but I can't tell you how often users hit them or how much each one costs in drop-off. If I did this again, I'd ship that tracking on day one, so the post-launch review is built on data rather than estimates.

The honest version: I can describe every edge case in detail, but I can't rank them by impact. That's the gap I'd close first given more time — not more design, just better visibility into what's already there.
