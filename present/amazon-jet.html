01 · The Brief

A One-Line User Story with a Business Model Behind It

The trigger wasn't a design brief. It was a support ticket pattern: Just Eat+ members who were also Amazon Prime members, paying twice for the same delivery benefit without realising it.

That one line implied the whole brief: two authentication systems, two loyalty ledgers, and a refund policy question that had to be answered before a single screen got designed.

Underneath it sat the real shape of the project — two global brands, three markets, and one activation moment that had to work identically whether someone arrived from Amazon's app, Amazon's website, or a Just Eat account they'd had for six years.

"As a Just Eat+ member and an Amazon Prime member, I want to switch to my free Prime delivery benefit — and I want to be clear on the refund policy so I can make an informed decision."
Field note that started the account‑linking initiative
01 · The Brief — Tensions

Three Problems Hiding Inside One User Story

Tension 01 · Two Identity Systems, One Moment
Amazon's single‑URL global login met Just Eat's per‑market URL structure — built for a different era of the product, never designed to hand off mid‑session to a partner.
Tension 02 · Three Markets, One Flow
Germany, Austria and Spain each had their own login quirks and legacy screens. One activation flow had to absorb that inconsistency without the user ever seeing the seams.
Tension 03 · Generosity vs. Exploitation
The benefit had to be trivially easy to claim once — and impossible to claim twice, across guest sessions, duplicate accounts and cross‑app identities.
02 · The Trade-off

Amazon's Login Met Just Eat's Markets. Something Had to Give.

Amazon authenticates through one global URL. Just Eat authenticates per market. There was no version of this flow where both architectures won — so the job was choosing which one bent, on purpose, and being able to defend it in a room with both companies in it.

Decision Log
Problem
A Prime member's single global session doesn't know which Just Eat market they belong to — Germany, Austria and Spain each run on separate login stacks.
Decision
Add one explicit country‑selection step over silent auto‑detect — a support‑ticket generator dressed up as convenience.
Trade-off Accepted
One additional screen in the happy path, in exchange for a login flow that never guesses on the user's behalf.
How It Was Communicated
I communicated that cost explicitly to both Amazon's and Just Eat's stakeholders before it shipped — not after someone noticed the extra tap in a usability read‑out.

04 · Flow Stages 1 & 2

Account Linking → Benefit Activation

Stage 1 — Account Linking (Steps 1–6)
Amazon credentials are verified and the user's market identity is confirmed. The country‑selector happens here — the deliberate extra step before hand-off.
Each step was mapped across Amazon web, JET web and native app lanes — the same logic, three surfaces, no forking.
Stage 2 — Benefit Activation (Steps 7–11)
The benefit is named, scoped and confirmed before it's applied. Nothing activates by default — every user gets one explicit confirmation moment.
Duplicate‑activation prevention is enforced here. Cross-app identities, guest sessions and duplicate accounts all resolve in this stage — not in a post-activation support queue.
04 · Flow Stage 3

Returning to the App — Where the Edge Cases Live

Step 12 — Is the App Installed & Signed In?
Yes: Straight to account‑match check.

No: App Store / Play Store, then native login.

Edge case: Users without the app are sent to install it — a deliberate bet on native CX over avoiding one extra hop.
Step 13 — Same Account as Activated?
Yes: Falls straight through to success state.

No: Guest-in-app or different logged-in account — both warned before a re-auth is forced.

Edge case: Activate on web with one identity, open the app signed into another. Tested for — not discovered in production.
Step 14 — Benefits Unlocked
An in‑app banner, not a full‑screen takeover — the user is dropped straight back into browsing restaurants, benefit already live.
05 · Leading Through It
"Three designers were embedded across five squads. Every one of them was under pressure from both sides of the partnership at once."
I Took the Pressure, Not My Team
Amazon's PMs wanted faster turnaround; internal stakeholders wanted more polish. I ran the weekly Amazon stakeholder syncs myself, bringing decisions with rationale and options — so my designers weren't defending their work in rooms they hadn't been part of.
Coherence Over Individual Correctness
Each designer's decisions were locally sensible. The risk was an experience that fragmented across squads. A fortnightly sync — decisions made, decisions pending, one dependency to resolve — kept the seams from showing.
Protecting the Security Model Was a Design Call
The country‑selector trade‑off wasn't handed down by engineering — it was a design decision I made and then had to sell to both companies, in writing, before it shipped.

07 · What I'd Change Next

Shipping It Taught Me Where the Seams Still Show

A few of these were conscious phase-1 trade-offs. With activation volume behind us, they read differently — as the next brief, not as mistakes.

01
Replace Generic Errors with Account-Specific Diagnostics
With real failure data now available, it's worth telling a user which branch they actually hit — not "something went wrong."
02
Unify Just Eat's Per-Market Login Screens
This flow absorbed the fragmentation rather than fixing it — the right call under a three-month deadline, but every future partner integration inherits the same legacy debt.
03
Soften the Guest-Account Warning into a Merge
A lighter merge flow — keep the basket, add the real account — would likely convert more of the DE/AT guest population, who hit this branch often.
04
Instrument Every Decision Node, Not Just the Happy Path
Next time, funnel instrumentation ships with v1 — so "what I'd change" is a dashboard, not a guess.
07 · Reflection
"I can tell you with confidence what each edge case does. I can't yet tell you which one costs the most conversions, because we didn't instrument the edges as tightly as the happy path. That gap is exactly what I'd fix first with another three months."
Shipping is not the finish line — it's when the real measurement begins.
